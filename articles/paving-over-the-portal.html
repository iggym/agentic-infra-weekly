Paving Over the Portal

Developer Enablement · Week 17, 2026 · Why your portal is the new ticket queue

In 2020, Spotify's platform team counted something that should have stopped them cold: 150+ internal tools scattered across the engineering organization, each with its own URL, its own login, its own mental model. Engineers building one of the world's most sophisticated audio delivery systems were spending more time navigating tooling than writing production code. Every new service required a tour through a dozen dashboards — CI config here, deployment pipeline there, monitoring setup somewhere else. The platform team had built a developer experience that was, in practice, a travel agency for infrastructure.

Spotify's answer was Backstage — but the critical detail most people miss is that Backstage succeeded not because it was a portal but because it was a plugin system that made the portal disappear. The plugin architecture meant capabilities surfaced inside existing workflows rather than requiring engineers to navigate to them. You didn't go to Backstage to find your CI pipeline; Backstage injected your CI status into the context where you were already working (Spotify Engineering Blog). This distinction — portal versus plugin, destination versus infrastructure — is the difference between a platform that enables and a platform that adds another tab.

Paved Road Rules

The developer portal is the new ticket queue. Real enablement means engineers never have to visit your platform team's UI at all.

Netflix's approach to platform engineering was the paved road: a set of opinionated libraries and tooling that embedded infrastructure decisions directly into the build process. When a Netflix engineer added Eureka for service discovery or Hystrix for circuit breaking, they weren't filling out a form in a portal. They were importing a library that encoded Netflix's infrastructure patterns into their application code. The paved road worked because it was invisible — it was the path of least resistance, and it was the path of least resistance by design (Netflix Tech Blog).

Netflix's stack ran on AWS infrastructure with Kubernetes on EKS, deployed through Spinnaker, and observed through Atlas. The Netflix OSS libraries — Eureka, Hystrix, Ribbon, Archaius — were the paved road. They weren't a separate product engineers had to discover and adopt; they were the default dependencies in every new service template. The platform team didn't need a portal because the platform was embedded in the build.

Then the stack grew more complex. Kubernetes brought new configuration surfaces. Service mesh added new abstractions. The paved road became harder to maintain as a single coherent path. Engineers started asking for a portal. The platform team built one. And adoption — measured by time-to-production for new services — slowed down. The portal had become a new coordination point, a new place to wait, a new queue.

The systems-thinking methodology at work here is progressive delivery with GitOps reconciliation: infrastructure changes should be declarative, version-controlled, and automatically reconciled. Netflix's original paved road embodied this principle implicitly — the library was the declaration, the build was the reconciliation. The portal broke this loop by introducing a human coordination step between declaration and reconciliation.

The Visible Queue

The pattern is everywhere. The CNCF Platforms White Paper, published by the CNCF Platforms Working Group, explicitly warns against building platforms that become "ticket queues with a UI." The paper documented a pattern observed across multiple organizations: platform teams build self-service portals, developers fill out forms, and the forms create tickets that platform engineers still have to process. The portal is a UI on top of the same bottleneck.

On Hacker News, discussions about internal developer portals consistently surface the same pattern from engineers at companies ranging from fintech startups to Fortune 500 enterprises: a portal is built, adoption is measured by page views, and the platform team celebrates traffic growth without recognizing that every page view is a developer who couldn't self-serve through their existing workflow.

At Coinbase, the platform engineering team documented their journey from a ticket-based model to self-service. Their initial self-service portal was, by their own assessment, a ticket queue with a UI — engineers filled out forms for infrastructure provisioning, and the platform team processed them in the same order they always had (Coinbase Engineering Blog). The real transformation came when they moved to Terraform modules and ArgoCD application sets — opinionated, declarative configurations that engineers could pull into their repositories without visiting any portal at all.

Coinbase's architecture tells the story: Kubernetes on EKS for infrastructure, ArgoCD with GitOps for orchestration, Terraform for provisioning, Helm for packaging, Kafka and Postgres and Redis for data, Datadog and PagerDuty for observability, Vault and OPA/Gatekeeper and AWS IAM for security. Every capability in this stack is accessible through a Terraform module or a Helm chart. The platform team's job is to maintain these modules, not to process tickets. Engineers pull the module, fill in the variables, and the GitOps reconciliation loop handles the rest. The platform is the pavement, not the gate.

The systems-thinking methodology here is defense-in-depth combined with GitOps reconciliation: the Terraform modules encode security policies, the OPA/Gatekeeper policies enforce them at admission time, and the ArgoCD reconciliation loop ensures drift is corrected. The platform team doesn't need to review each change because the policy enforcement is automated. The engineer doesn't need to visit a portal because the platform is embedded in their git workflow.

The portal is the problem it was built to solve.

The Counterargument

The strongest counterargument: "Portals provide a necessary discovery layer. Engineers need to know what capabilities exist before they can use them." Discovery is a documentation problem, not a portal problem — if your engineers need a portal to find your capabilities, your capabilities are already too fragmented to use effectively. The answer is fewer, more opinionated capabilities surfaced through IDE autocomplete and CLI help text, not a portal with a search bar.

The Path Forward

  • Audit your platform's touchpoints. Count how many separate UIs an engineer must visit to go from "I need infrastructure" to "I'm running in production." If it's more than zero, you have a portal problem. Every UI is a context switch, and every context switch is a tax on developer productivity.
  • Make your paved path a CLI command, not a web form. Use PathCheck to scan your existing Terraform and Helm configs and identify where developers are going off-road. The tool uses LLM analysis to compare your actual configs against your defined golden paths, surfacing deviations before they become incidents.
  • Replace your portal's forms with IDE-native config generation. Use ConfigBrew to generate paved-path Terraform and Helm manifests from natural language descriptions. Instead of filling out a form that creates a ticket, your engineers describe what they need and get a production-ready config that follows your platform's standards.
  • Measure platform adoption by what disappears, not what appears. If your portal's analytics show increasing page views, that's not adoption — that's dependency. The metric that matters is time-to-production for new services, and the only way to improve it is to remove steps, not add them.

Start Now

Open your developer portal's analytics. Find the single most-visited page. That page is your most important paved path to automate away. Replace it with a CLI command, a Terraform module, or a Helm chart — something an engineer can use without leaving their terminal. Do it this week.
