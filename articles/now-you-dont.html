Now You Don't

Why your developer platform's self-service is actually a new bottleneck — and the teams that made it disappear

Sometime in 2019, Spotify's platform team looked at a number that made them uncomfortable: new engineers were taking weeks to deploy their first service. Not days. Weeks. The problem wasn't a lack of tools — Spotify had hundreds of those. The problem was that no one could find them. Documentation lived in Confluence wikis, GitHub READMEs, and the heads of senior engineers who were tired of answering the same questions. The platform team's answer was Backstage — a unified developer portal that would become one of the most-adopted open-source projects in the CNCF ecosystem. But the detail that gets less airtime is what happened after: Backstage became another place engineers had to go. The portal solved discovery. It didn't solve cognitive load. Engineers still had to visit the portal, search for the right template, fill out a form, and then return to their IDE to actually build something. The catalog was a better map. But engineers still had to navigate it themselves.

Missing the Forest

Here's the pattern that connects Spotify's Backstage, Netflix's Paved Road, and the DORA team's 2024 findings: platform teams are optimizing for the wrong metric. They measure adoption — how many engineers visited the portal, how many templates were clicked, how many services are registered. They should measure absence. The best platform is the one nobody visits, because it's already woven into the work they were doing anyway. Every "self-service portal" is a confession that your platform is a destination, not a utility. You don't visit your electrical grid. You don't log into your water supply. The infrastructure that works best is the infrastructure you never think about.

Connect the Dots

Netflix understood this earlier than most. Their Paved Road initiative — documented extensively in their engineering blog and conference talks — didn't build a portal. It built a set of opinionated defaults that made the right thing the easiest thing. When you created a new microservice at Netflix, the default option was already production-ready, observable, and resilient. The paved path wasn't a page you visited; it was the path you were already on unless you deliberately chose to leave it. The critical architectural decision was making platform capabilities available through the tools engineers already used — their IDE, their CLI, their CI pipeline — rather than through a separate web interface. Their circuit-breaker pattern and chaos engineering practice ensured that the paved path was also the most reliable path. The easiest thing and the right thing were the same thing.

The DORA 2024 Accelerate State of DevOps Report confirmed this pattern with data spanning hundreds of organizations. Teams that treated their internal platform as a product — with real SLAs, dedicated product managers, and user feedback loops — saw significantly higher developer productivity than teams that treated platforms as internal tooling projects. But the report's subtler finding is the one that matters: the productive teams didn't measure success by portal adoption. They measured it by cognitive load reduction. The portal was a means, not an end, and many teams had confused the two.

Spotify's own evolution reflects this. After open-sourcing Backstage, they continued investing in CLI tools and IDE plugins that made the portal's capabilities available without visiting the web interface. The portal was the catalog. The real platform was the automation that made the catalog unnecessary for most daily workflows.

A platform that requires a visit is a platform that's already failed.

The Flip Side

The strongest counterargument is real: portals provide discoverability and governance. Engineers need to know what services exist, what templates are available, and what compliance requirements apply. Without a portal, how do they find anything? This is valid — but it confuses the catalog with the platform. Discoverability is a documentation problem, not a platform problem. And governance that requires a human to visit a portal and click a button is governance that can be automated. Netflix's Paved Road didn't eliminate discoverability — it made the default path discoverable by default, so you only needed the catalog when you were doing something unusual. The catalog should be the exception path, not the happy path. If your governance requires a visit, your governance is a toll booth, not a guardrail.

Your Move

1. Audit required visits

Every place a developer must go to your platform instead of having it come to them is a friction point. Count them. Track them. Set a quarterly target to eliminate half. Tools like Friction Log can help you systematically identify and categorize where your platform adds friction instead of removing it — logging every moment of cognitive load in the tools engineers already use.

2. Deliver through existing tools

IDE extensions that surface the right template. CLI tools that scaffold the compliant path. CI pipelines that auto-apply the right policies. The platform should meet engineers where they are, not the other way around. Pathfinder can analyze your CI/CD pipelines to show you exactly where the "paved path" diverges from what developers actually do — so you can close the gap.

3. Change your metrics

Stop measuring portal adoption. Start measuring time-to-first-deploy, number of context switches, and developer satisfaction with platform capabilities. The DORA team's research shows these are the metrics that actually predict productivity gains. Service Radar can scan your service catalog to identify "dead zones" — services nobody uses, documentation that's stale, golden paths that nobody follows — so you measure what's actually working, not what's just registered.

First Things First

Open your platform's analytics dashboard today. Look at the "most visited pages" report. Every page on that list is a place where your platform is asking engineers to come to it instead of meeting them where they already are. Pick the top one. Make it unnecessary within 30 days.

Recall Practice

What metric should platform teams measure instead of portal adoption?
Absence — how many engineers never need to visit the portal because capabilities are already woven into their workflow.
What was the key architectural decision in Netflix's Paved Road?
Making platform capabilities available through the tools engineers already used — IDE, CLI, CI pipeline — rather than through a separate web interface.
What did the DORA 2024 report find about platform engineering?
Teams treating their platform as a product with real SLAs saw significantly higher developer productivity than teams treating platforms as internal tooling.
Why is a "self-service portal" a confession of platform failure?
Because it means the platform is a destination engineers must visit, not a utility woven into their existing workflow.
What three actions should platform teams take to make their platform invisible?
Audit required visits, deliver capabilities through existing tools, and change metrics from adoption to cognitive load reduction.
