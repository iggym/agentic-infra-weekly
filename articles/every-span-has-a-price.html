Every Span Has a Price

Oct 7, 2026 · 5 min read

The Sixty-Five Million Dollar Mystery

In May 2023, Datadog told investors on an earnings call that a large upfront bill from one customer wouldn't repeat. The industry immediately started guessing who it was and how big the bill had been. Gergely Orosz of The Pragmatic Engineer tracked it down[1]. The customer was Coinbase, and the bill was about $65 million for observability in 2021.

The story behind it was not mysterious. After Coinbase's 2021 public listing, growth was the only priority. Nobody was watching infrastructure costs closely, and telemetry grew with everything else. When crypto markets turned in 2022, the bill suddenly mattered. Coinbase set up a team to move to a self-hosted stack built on Grafana, Prometheus, and ClickHouse. Datadog responded with a much better deal, and Coinbase stayed[1].

That was a crypto exchange running conventional microservices. Now consider what happens to telemetry when the workload is an AI agent. One user request might trigger a planning call to a model, a dozen tool calls, several retrieval queries, a few retries, and a final summarization call, each producing its own spans and logs. Some teams also log every prompt and response in full.

The Coinbase bill was a warning about telemetry growing without a budget. Agents make it grow faster.

Telemetry Has Unit Economics

Telemetry is a product with a unit cost, not a free byproduct. Agents multiply spans per request, so sampling by value is now an architecture decision, not a billing afterthought.

Most teams think of observability as exhaust: something the system gives off that you capture and keep. The default is to collect everything and sort it out later. The cost shows up in a separate budget, usually owned by someone who didn't choose what to instrument.

That model breaks when the cost per request jumps by an order of magnitude. Telemetry needs the same treatment as any other product line: a unit cost (dollars per thousand requests), a value it delivers (incidents caught, questions answered), and a design that matches the two.

If you can't say what a span is worth, you can't say whether to keep it.

Sampling Is Capacity Planning

Distributed tracing already has the tool for this: sampling. OpenTelemetry's documentation describes the two main approaches[2]. Head sampling decides at the start of a request whether to record the trace, for example keeping 10% at random. It's cheap and simple, but it can't know in advance which requests will turn out interesting. Tail sampling waits until the trace is complete and then decides, so it can keep every error and every slow request and drop most of the healthy, fast ones.

For agents, tail sampling maps almost directly onto what you care about. The traces worth keeping are the ones where something unusual happened: a tool call failed, the agent looped, a retry fired, latency or token use spiked, or a guardrail triggered. The OpenTelemetry Collector's tail-sampling processor supports policies on exactly these conditions: status codes, latency thresholds, span attributes, and rate limits, combined with a small probabilistic baseline[3].

The hidden connection is capacity planning. SREs don't provision every service for infinite load. They decide what load matters and size for it. Sampling is the same decision applied to observability: decide which questions you need to answer, and keep the data that answers them.

There's also a second axis: what each span carries. OpenTelemetry's generative-AI semantic conventions define standard attributes for model calls, token counts, and tool executions, and they treat capturing full prompt and response content as opt-in rather than the default[4]. That's both a privacy choice and a cost choice. Prompt bodies are often the largest thing in the span.

The Evidence

The cost side is on the record. Coinbase's observability spend reached about $65 million in a single year, driven by growth with no cost owner, according to The Pragmatic Engineer's reporting[1]. The same reporting notes that the credible threat of moving to an open-source stack is what brought the price down. Cost discipline came from the architecture, not from asking the vendor nicely.

The tooling side is mature. Head and tail sampling are documented OpenTelemetry concepts[2]. The Collector's tail-sampling processor is an established component with composable policies[3]. The generative-AI semantic conventions give a shared vocabulary for model and tool spans, so sampling rules can target them consistently[4].

The methods at work: unit economics makes cost per request visible. Tail-based sampling keeps anomalies and drops repetition. Attribute budgets keep span payloads small by default, with full content only on demand.

Grain of Salt

The counterargument: sampling throws away data you'll wish you had. Agent failures are often subtle (a bad answer with no error and normal latency), and you can't tail-sample on a signal you don't have. Storage is also getting cheaper, and columnar backends make high-volume telemetry affordable.

The first point is real, and it's an argument for better signals, not for keeping everything. Attach a quality score, an eval result, or a user's thumbs-down to the trace, and those become sampling keys too. The second point helps but doesn't change the shape of the problem. Cheaper storage lowers the price per span, and agents raise the number of spans. Without a budget, the second effect wins.

Put a Price Tag on It

  1. Compute telemetry cost per thousand requests. Take last month's observability bill, split it by service, and divide by request volume. Do the same for your agent services separately. That number is your baseline.
  2. Count spans per request. For a typical agent request, count spans and log lines, and compare with a typical non-agent request. That ratio tells you how fast agent traffic will grow your bill.
  3. Adopt the GenAI semantic conventions. Standardize model, token, and tool attributes across services so sampling and cost rules apply consistently[4]. Leave prompt and response content capture off by default.
  4. Move to tail sampling for agent traces. In the OpenTelemetry Collector, keep 100% of errors, slow traces, and traces with retries or guardrail events, plus a small random baseline, and drop the rest[3].
  5. Give telemetry an owner and a budget. Put cost per thousand requests on the same dashboard as latency and error rate, and review it in the same meeting.

First 24 Hours

Open your tracing backend and pull up one complete trace for a single agent request. Count the spans. Then check what share of last week's agent traces contained an error, a retry, or a latency outlier. If it's under 5%, you're paying full price to store 95% of traces that nobody will ever open.
