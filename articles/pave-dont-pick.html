Pave, Don't Pick

Why most developer platforms make engineers slower — and the teams who actually ship fast never touch the portal

Week 23 · June 2026 Developer Enablement
Self-Service Portal Invisible Platform

Old model: give engineers more choices. New model: give them fewer decisions.

In 2016, a Spotify engineer sat down to deploy a new microservice. She opened her browser and faced a wall: fifteen separate internal tools, each with its own login, its own documentation, and its own opinion about how things should work. She wasn't new — she'd been at Spotify for two years. But the infrastructure had grown faster than the tooling to manage it. By the time Spotify's platform team cataloged the sprawl, they counted over a hundred internal tools. Each one built to solve a real problem. Each one adding a new decision a developer had to make before writing a single line of product code.

This wasn't a Spotify problem. It was the industry's problem, wearing a Spotify t-shirt.

The Choice Trap

Here's the pattern: a company's infrastructure gets complex. Someone builds a portal to unify it. The portal lists every tool, every option, every configuration. It's "self-service." Engineers can now choose from all the things. And they do — slowly, inconsistently, and often incorrectly.

The Bold Reframe: developer platforms that accelerate shipping don't give engineers more choices. They give them fewer decisions. The fastest teams don't have better portals. They have better defaults that make the platform invisible.

Spotify's Backstage became the most widely adopted developer portal in the world — not because it was a great portal, but because of something quieter: its Software Templates. These templates were opinionated scaffolds that let a developer spin up a new service with the right CI/CD pipeline, the right monitoring, the right Kubernetes config — all pre-baked. You didn't choose your monitoring stack. You didn't choose your deployment strategy. The template chose for you. And that was the point.

The Spotify platform team's layered architecture tells the story: infrastructure runs on Google Kubernetes Engine; orchestration uses Helm charts distributed via Backstage Software Templates; the runtime layer connects to Google Cloud Pub/Sub and BigQuery; observability runs through Google Cloud Operations Suite and OpenTelemetry; governance uses Google Cloud IAM with custom service-to-service mTLS. Each layer is a decision a developer no longer has to make. The template encodes the right answer for each one.

Hidden Wiring

The connection most teams miss is between cognitive load and velocity. Team Topologies — the methodology defined by Matthew Skelton and Manuel Pais — explicitly names cognitive load as the limiting factor on team performance. A platform team's job isn't to build a catalog of tools. It's to reduce the cognitive load on stream-aligned teams so they can focus on the domain they serve.

Adidas adopted this approach in their platform engineering journey, presented at KubeCon EU 2023. Their platform team built on Kubernetes with ArgoCD for GitOps and Backstage as the developer portal. But the critical insight from their talk wasn't about the portal. It was about the golden paths — opinionated, pre-approved routes through the infrastructure that a developer could follow without thinking about what lay beneath. The platform team's measure of success wasn't portal page views. It was time-to-first-deploy for a new engineer.

Zalando's platform engineering team took this even further. Their internal developer platform, built on Kubernetes on AWS EKS, was designed so that engineers interact with Git and CI/CD, not with Kubernetes directly. The platform team's principle was explicit: if a developer needs to understand Kubernetes to deploy a service, the platform has failed. They use ArgoCD for GitOps reconciliation and custom admission controllers for governance. The developer's workflow is: write code, push to Git, get a running service. The platform handles the rest.

The systems-thinking methodology at work here is the GitOps reconciliation loop — the platform continuously converges the actual state toward the desired state, so developers don't need to intervene. Combined with defense-in-depth (admission controllers, IAM policies, network policies), the platform enforces the right behavior without requiring the developer to think about it.

The best platform is the one you never open.

Lock-In or Lift-Off

"Paved paths are just lock-in with a friendly name. Engineers need flexibility to solve novel problems."

Fair — if your paved path is a cage. But the teams that get this right build paved paths with escape hatches. Spotify's Backstage templates are forkable. If a team needs something the template doesn't provide, they can eject and customize. But the default is fast. The override exists when you need it. The key design principle: make the right thing the easy thing, and make the custom thing possible. Most teams, most of the time, never need the escape hatch.

This is the same principle behind GitOps reconciliation loops in ArgoCD — you can manually override, but the system will keep pulling you back toward the desired state. The same principle behind progressive delivery and canary rollouts in SRE practice — you can deploy directly, but the safe path is the default. The same principle behind idempotency in infrastructure-as-code — you can run your Terraform apply repeatedly, and the result converges. The pattern is consistent: reduce the default decision surface, preserve the escape route.

Ship by Tuesday

1. Audit your decision surface. Count the number of choices a developer makes to deploy a new service. If it's more than five, you have a golden-path opportunity. Use FrictionLens to measure cognitive load across your repos — the free tier covers one repository, which is enough to spot the pattern.

2. Build one golden path, not a portal. Pick the most common service type in your organization and create a single, opinionated template that deploys it with monitoring, CI/CD, and security pre-configured. Use PathForge to generate the scaffold from your best-performing service — the free tier gives you three template generations.

3. Measure time-to-first-deploy, not portal adoption. Your platform's success metric is how fast a new engineer can ship their first change, not how many people log into your portal. Use PaveCheck to compare velocity on golden-path vs. non-golden-path services — the free tier gives you one comparison, which is enough to prove the concept.
